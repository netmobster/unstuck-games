The case against: does the model eat the limits too?

This issue argues that the operator's job looks less like supervising every output and more like setting limits and auditing them; this piece argues that the model is coming for the limits too.

In the September monthly's words, this issue's argument is that the operator's job going into October "looks less like supervising every output and more like setting limits, then auditing them". It is the tidiest reading of the month, and the kindest to the operator. This piece reads the month harder, and its hardest step is its own: the model eats the limits too.

Rule IVA steelman says it's a steelman: written in-house, to win.

One step past Mollick

Two of the month's five claims point the same way: checking moved to machines, and the wrapper got thinner while OpenAI moved in on routing.

05 Who checks the checker? →Checking moved to machines.

By the bitter lesson, Ethan Mollick argues, the model eats the wrapper: "organizing work is just one more thing AI can learn to do". He stops there. In his account the agents organise and people choose the target, a division he says "seems right, at least for now". Agents may fit into firms more easily than he expected, "as long as humans are guiding them in the right direction". That is close to this issue's own answer.

The step past him is this piece's, and it starts from his "for now". On its reading, a guardrail is a limit written in prose, and the model has been eating those. The monthly keeps the two apart, deleting instructions and adding limits, yet its own deletion pass cuts the guardrails a model no longer needs.

Cursor made a cut like that on 23 September, trimming roughly 66% of its system prompt. In its words: "As models improved, much of that direction became unnecessary." This piece reads much of that direction as guardrails. Anthropic had cut 80% of Claude Code's system prompt, as reported on 22 August. Its Thariq Shihipar says the core harness has to be complex and very secure, yet "in the limit, Claude.md goes away".

On that reading, the line between a guardrail and a limit is whether the model can be trusted to keep it, and better models will keep moving it. Nothing in September's record shows a spend cap or a sandbox made redundant; the monthly adds both. That they go next is a bet, and this piece makes it.

The cost objection

The honest objection is cost: the swarm Mollick describes burned 130 billion output tokens, about $40M at public prices by Latent Space's estimate. September didn't answer it. The frontier held at $10/$50, and AINews found GPT-6 Astra 2.5 times pricier per token than its predecessor (a paid post, checked as far as its public preview). The cuts came below the frontier: GPT-6 Sol's price halved, Luna's output price fell 58% and Opus 5.5 came in 20% cheaper.

~$40M130 billion output tokens at public prices, for the swarm Mollick describes (Latent Space's estimate)

So this piece can't promise the swarm gets cheap on a schedule, only say who moves the price. Ramp's index showed AI spend falling while usage rose about 50% since July, and The Decoder put nearly all of the fall down to OpenAI and Anthropic competing. Betting on the bitter lesson is betting the frontier follows: a bet on the vendors, not the operator.

Is the machine the better gate?

The issue keeps one human step: sign-off on agent actions that are irreversible or leave the building. In Anthropic's study (see 05), testers caught a swapped-in dangerous command 13.6% of the time and the classifier 89%.

The best evidence against this section is in the post that reported the study: users reject 39% of the plans Claude puts to them for approval, but only 3% of individual permission requests. Perhaps people push back when they see the whole job, and the study never tested a short gate list. That is a fair hit. But a rejection rate isn't a catch rate: it counts refusals, not whether the refused plans were the dangerous ones. The only human catch rate on record is the testers', about one in six even early in a session.

39% · 3%of plans rejected · of individual requests rejected · refusals, not catches

Classifiers miss too. Auto mode let 11% through in the same study and, after hardening, still missed 7% of a red-team attack set; Anthropic still recommends reviewing high-stakes production changes yourself. So this section moves the gatekeeper, not the gate. But a gate on what can't be undone belongs to whichever checker misses less, and in the only test on record, the testers missed nearly nine in ten.

The audit may be next. The monthly's first example of "the human step moves up a level" is a DeepMind swarm in which, after one agent gamed an automated grader, agents audited agents. And audits need something to see: OpenAI found a June breach of a New South Wales fire-data service only on 29 September.

The model and its sellers set the bill

The issue's example of a limit done right is Databricks putting Astra on its own sub-budget. After the Astra rollout, coding spend rose 60%, gains were clear only on highly complex work, and simpler coding may not have improved much. Then came the sub-budget. On the monthly's reading, the model set the cost and the outcome first. The limit was the receipt.

On AINews's figures (a paid post, checked as far as its public preview), Gemini 4 Argon tied Astra on score and burned more than twice the output tokens to get there; 07 has the bill. On the benchmark, Google set the price and the model set the token count; the operator set neither. A spend cap, the issue's most concrete limit, is a ceiling on a number they set.

Somebody else sets the limits

The limits with teeth came from a lab and a legislature. OpenAI cancelled GPT-6.1 Astra because it fell short on "scope and authorization", in the words of Saachi Jain, its head of safety systems. In civil suits, California's AB 316 has barred the defence that the AI acted autonomously since 1 January.

Between 28 September and 1 October, a state, a non-profit, a regulator, California's attorney general and two senators acted or went public: filings, a subpoena, a confirmed probe, a hearing and a bill with no text. No liability has been assigned yet. The bill, as reported, targets anyone who knowingly operates an agent that recklessly causes hacking damage; announced on 1 October, it had no bill number or published text as of 5 October. If it passes as reported, it will be a limit on the operator, written by somebody else.

On this piece's reading, then, the model takes the work beneath the limits, and labs and lawmakers set the limits above them. On that reading, the operator's share is narrower than this issue says, and shrinking from both ends.

That's the case, made as hard as the evidence allows and one step harder. Over to the editor: will the model eat these limits too?
