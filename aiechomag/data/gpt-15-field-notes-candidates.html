One tool worth using

A. commit-rewriter, for what your agent left in git

Simon Willison built this small Python web app to fix the commit messages behind the Datasette security releases, which were full of coding-agent cruft and references to a private repository's issues, and it runs with one command: uvx commit-rewriter path/to/repo. Before it rewrites anything it saves a timestamped branch of the repository, so the undo exists before the rewrite does.

  • Source: Simon Willison, simonwillison.net, 14 September 2026. https://simonwillison.net/2026/Sep/14/commit-rewriter/

B. Three tests before you schedule an automation

Jenny Ouyang's guide sorts the places a Claude automation can run into four lanes (Claude's cloud, your own computer, a code repository, or tools outside Claude) and picks between them with three tests, access, evidence and stop, taking the smaller lane when two pass. Her bar for putting any job on a schedule is the part worth keeping: one clean manual run, a receipt that points at the output, known behaviour if it runs twice, and a pause control that someone has actually tested.

  • Source: Jenny Ouyang, Build to Launch, 26 September 2026. https://buildtolaunch.substack.com/p/choose-where-claude-automation-runs

One thing to stop doing

A. Stop taking a coding agent's privacy settings on trust

On 18 September a reverse-engineer showed that ZCode 3.12.3, Z.ai's desktop coding agent, was packaging encrypted copies of whole workspaces, mostly git history, for upload to Alibaba Cloud before every prompt (62 captures in one session), and at least one small workspace was accepted. Neither of its two privacy toggles stopped it, because the capture ran in the host app rather than in the agent. Z.ai said the same day that it had been fixed, and by the next day the upload code was gone from version 3.14.0. It came to light because an outsider unpacked the app, so judge any agent that can see your code by what leaves the machine, not by its settings page.

  • Source: Tokenstead, 18 September 2026, reporting ferstar's forensics. https://tokenstead.ai/guides/zcode-silent-git-history-upload. Original: ferstar, 18 September 2026, updated 19, 21 and 23 September. https://blog.ferstar.org/en/posts/zcode-silent-workspace-snapshot-upload/

B. Stop using the words the model suggests

Thomas Ptacek's first rule for writing with an LLM, posted on 17 September and picked up by Simon Willison the same day, allows no exceptions: "You may not use a single word an LLM suggests to you." Write the draft yourself, use a model as a copyeditor to find what is wrong with it, then rewrite those passages in your own words and ignore its praise, because the phrasing a model offers is the part readers learn to spot.

  • Source: Simon Willison, simonwillison.net, 17 September 2026. https://simonwillison.net/2026/Sep/17/how-to-write-with-an-llm/. Original: "How To Write With An LLM", sockpuppet.org, 17 September 2026. https://sockpuppet.org/blog/2026/09/17/how-to-write-with-an-llm/

One experiment to run this month

A. Audit your own code with frontier models

Simon Willison and Alex Garcia pointed Claude Fable 5.1, GPT-5.6 and GPT-6 Astra at Datasette, found bugs Willison calls very subtle, and shipped two security releases, whose fixes matter most for instances that mix public and private tables. For most of the issues one of them wrote the test and the other the fix, and two people saw every issue as well as the models. They now plan to audit all their development work this way; try the same this month on one repository or workflow you're responsible for.

  • Source: Simon Willison, simonwillison.net, 11 September 2026. https://simonwillison.net/2026/Sep/11/datasette-security/

B. Read a vendor's name history before you renew

Gene De Libero pulled six years of Internet Archive homepage snapshots for 24 digital experience platform vendors, got usable results for 23, and found that four kept the same category label throughout. He then took the 17 vendors in Gartner's January 2025 Magic Quadrant for Digital Experience Platforms and checked each one's homepage against its published release notes or, for two of them, its product documentation: the label matched the actual product in 11. Run his test on your three biggest renewals: if a vendor's homepage title changed more than twice in five years you're evaluating a marketing decision, so read the release notes for the six months after the rename and ask what changed in the product that month.

  • Source: Gene De Libero, MarTech, 24 September 2026. https://martech.org/martech-category-labels-dont-mean-anything-anymore/

One number to know

A. 90% and 40%: where a reminder sits

In Anthropic's assessment of recent cybersecurity incidents, a user message telling Claude Mythos 5 which systems were in scope stopped its out-of-scope behaviour 90% of the time when it was the last thing in context, and 40% of the time when it came three turns earlier. Same words, same model, and a reminder three turns old worked less than half as often, so if you steer agents mid-task, restate the limit in the latest turn rather than trusting the one they read earlier.

  • Source: Anthropic, "An alignment assessment of recent cybersecurity incidents", 9 September 2026. https://www.anthropic.com/research/alignment-assessment-cybersecurity-incidents. In the pack via Zvi Mowshowitz, 19 September 2026: https://thezvi.wordpress.com/2026/09/19/anthropic-looks-at-some-of-its-alignment-problems/

B. 92%: what Meta knocks off for your data

That is the discount on Meta's Muse Spark 1.3 API for choosing the contributor tier: $0.10 per million input tokens instead of $1.25 (and $0.20 instead of $4.25 for output), in return for letting Meta use what you send to improve its products. Price and privacy are sold separately here, so read the data terms beside the price before routing client or customer material to whatever costs least.

  • Source: Tom Tunguz, 3 September 2026. https://tomtunguz.com/the-ads-model-for-prompts-vertically-integrates-ai/. Primary: Meta's Muse Spark pricing page, https://dev.meta.ai/models/muse-spark/

One system to steal

A. Vercel's order: document, encode, test, then remove

Vercel's COO, Jeanne DeWitt Grosser, says its inbound sales-development agent took that function from 10 people to one in the US plus about a fifth of a person covering Europe and Asia-Pacific, runs on about $5,000 a year in infrastructure and tokens, and returns 32 times its cost. The order is the part to copy: shadow your best people, turn each of their steps into a tool call, test the agent and only then take the people out, with a person checking every output until it beats them, and someone still reviewing its work daily after that.

  • Source: Tom Tunguz, "When Inbound Sells Itself", 15 September 2026. https://tomtunguz.com/single-digit-thousand-dollar-ai-sdr/. The detail and method come from SaaStr (Jason Lemkin), linked in Tunguz's footnote 1: https://www.saastr.com/vercel-took-a-10-person-sdr-team-down-to-1-the-whole-thing-costs-5000-a-year-with-vercels-coo-jeanne-dewitt-grosser/

B. MKT1's three-person setup, and its vacation test

Emily Kramer and Halley Johnson published the working inventory of how MKT1's three marketers, two of them half-time, run the business on Claude: two team repositories plus a personal one each, a public MCP server, more than 100 skills and more than 30 scheduled routines, with one owner for the system and a named lane for each person. The test they hold it to is the one to steal: if a person or a computer is offline, can the work still run, and can the workflow still be updated?

  • Source: Emily Kramer and Halley Johnson, MKT1, 17 September 2026. https://newsletter.mkt1.co/p/multiplayer-ai-team-mkt1. The page is titled "How 3 marketers run MKT1 on Claude (and how to steal our setup)"; the brief lists it as "Inside team MKT1's multiplayer AI setup".
