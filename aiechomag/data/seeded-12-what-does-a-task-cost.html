What does a task cost?

Checker test: the seeded copy, with its 6 planted errors

This copy contains 6 planted errors. It is the test copy, not the issue. The result is in the checker test.

By the end of September the sticker price no longer told the big labs apart, and the number that did was the cost of a finished task. [S: 01-the-monthly-september-2026.md#3, Why it matters]

In early September, The ECHO Files' weekly roundups said model choice had become the least consequential decision in the stack [S: 01-the-monthly-september-2026.md#3, Where it appeared; 04-checks/check-claim-3.md §2, Where it appeared]. By October the evidence said something narrower. The vendor mattered less. Which model got which job mattered more, and so did what was wrapped around it [S: 01-the-monthly-september-2026.md#intro; #4, Verdict]. What changed was the unit a bill is counted in: the cost of a finished task, rather than the price of a token.

The sticker converged

Prices were falling before the month began. OpenAI cut Luna's price 80% in July, while Anthropic held Opus at $5/$25 per million input and output tokens across five releases [S: 04-checks/check-claim-3.md §2, Background].

Then came the price war. On 22 September Anthropic released Claude Opus 5.5 at $4/$20, a 20% cut [S: 04-checks/check-claim-3.md §2, How it shifted and What I got wrong]. About an hour later OpenAI released GPT-6 Sol at $2/$10, half GPT-5.6 Sol's promotional $4/$20, and GPT-6 Luna at $0.10/$0.50 [S: 04-checks/check-claim-3.md §2, How it shifted; §1, Also worth knowing] [NEW: https://simonwillison.net/2026/Sep/22/opus-and-sol-and-luna/]. Between 28 and 30 September, Anthropic, OpenAI and Google each launched or announced a model at $2/$10: Claude Sonnet 5.5, GPT-6.1 Sol and Gemini 4 Argon [S: 04-checks/check-claim-3.md §1 item 1].

Two of the three labs were already there. Sonnet 5 had sold at $2/$10 since its launch, and Anthropic's pricing page says a planned rise to $3/$15 won't happen. GPT-6 Sol had been at $2/$10 for a week [S: 04-checks/check-claim-3.md §1 item 1]. Google's $2/$10 is an introductory half price, for trusted testers only [S: 04-checks/check-claim-3.md §1 item 1].

So the sticker stopped separating vendors [S: 01-the-monthly-september-2026.md#3, Why it matters], and the vendors began selling in other units. Anthropic's launch page said Opus 5.5 costs 40% less to run than Opus 5 on typical workloads [S: 04-checks/check-claim-3.md §2, What I got wrong]. That is a claim about work rather than tokens, and it is Anthropic's own; the list price fell 20%. Sonnet 5.5 kept its list price, and Anthropic says it costs up to 30% less per task because it needs fewer tokens [S: 04-checks/check-claim-3.md §2, How it shifted].

Argon's three prices

Gemini 4 Argon shows what a token price hides. It has three prices.

The first is on the announcement: $2/$10, a 50% introductory discount with no end date announced [S: 04-checks/check-claim-3.md §1 item 1]. Access starts with government users and trusted cyber defenders in Google's Fairwind Program. Developers, enterprises and consumers come later [S: 04-checks/check-claim-3.md §1 item 1].

The second is the standard price behind the discount: $4/$20 [S: 04-checks/check-claim-3.md §1 item 1].

The third is the one a bill is made of. Argon matched GPT-6 Astra at 53 on Artificial Analysis's Intelligence Index, but averaged 62,000 output tokens per task to Astra's 27,000 [S: 04-checks/check-claim-3.md §2, How it shifted]. Astra lists at $10/$50, so Argon's introductory price is a fifth of Astra's per token [S: 04-checks/check-claim-3.md §2, How it shifted]. AINews put the cost at $1.99 a task against Astra's $3.26 [S: 04-checks/check-claim-3.md §2, How it shifted; §1 item 1]; output tokens make up under half of each [NEW: https://www.latent.space/p/ainews-gemini-4-argon-gdms-answer]. At its standard price, Argon would come to $3.98 a task, more than Astra [S: 04-checks/check-claim-3.md §1 item 1].

The benchmark calls it a tie. The invoice doesn't. On these numbers Argon beats Astra per task only while the discount lasts, because it burns far more tokens per task (62,000 output tokens to Astra's 27,000) to reach the same score [S: 04-checks/check-claim-3.md §2, How it shifted]. The token price on the announcement is an input, not an answer.

Per job from the start

The month's founding evidence was already priced in work. On 3 September Latent Space put GPT-6 Astra at under $6 an hour: 38 tokens a second at a top rate of $50 per million tokens [S: 04-checks/check-claim-4.md §1 item 1; §2, Background and Why it matters]. The next day Artificial Analysis found Astra 2.5 times pricier per token than its predecessor, cheaper per task in some workflows and dearer in others [S: 04-checks/check-claim-3.md §1 item 5].

The comparisons that followed split by job. On 10 September Nate B Jones compared Claude Fable 5.1 and Astra under the title "Which Model Gets Which Job", and concluded that one project could give him reasons to use both [S: 04-checks/check-claim-3.md §2, How it shifted]. Zvi Mowshowitz planned to put every non-trivial question to both models for a while, because their answers to one hard question had complemented each other [S: 04-checks/check-claim-3.md §1 item 7]. On 22 September Claire Vo's blind test on How I AI split three ways. GPT-6 Sol won her over on clear writing, Opus 5.5 looked strongest for long-running agents and B2B front-end work, and Astra was her favourite [S: 04-checks/check-claim-3.md §1 item 6].

On 26 September Zvi published a job map: Opus 5.5 by default, Fable 5.1 for correctness in expert domains, Astra for ambitious coding and some science work, and Luna and other cheap models for budget work [S: 04-checks/check-claim-3.md §1 item 2]. That is a routing table, written as a blog post. By 3 October Jones was routing in practice, keeping Astra for the most demanding work and moving recurring jobs to Sol to save his plan allowance [S: 04-checks/check-claim-3.md §2, How it shifted].

The wrapper's number, and its limits

The other half of the bill is the wrapper. Alibaba.com's president, Kuo Zhang, wrote in Fortune on 9 September that work gets done by three things together: the model; a harness that equips it with tools and memory and lets it act; and context on what good looks like in a given industry [S: 04-checks/check-claim-4.md §1 item 4].

On 17 September Tom Tunguz wrote up HarnessTax, a study that ran the same models under different harnesses [S: 04-checks/check-claim-4.md §1 item 2]. His lead example was one switch. GPT-6 Sol moved from Claude Code to Pi, a minimal open-source harness, and its cost per resolved task fell from $1.540 to $0.441, a 71% cut [S: 04-checks/check-claim-4.md §2, How it shifted; 04-checks/check-claim-3.md §1 item 8]. That is larger than any September cut to input or output token prices [S: 04-checks/check-claim-4.md §1 item 3; 04-checks/check-claim-3.md §1, Also worth knowing]. Fable 5.1's cache reads did fall 75% at the start of the month, so the comparison holds for input and output prices only [S: 04-checks/check-claim-4.md §1 item 3].

The limits matter as much as the number. The 71% is one model moved to one harness, not an average [S: 04-checks/check-claim-4.md §2, What I got wrong]. HarnessTax's own headline, as GIGAZINE reported it, was that success rates didn't vary significantly while costs differed by up to five times, so the 71% isn't even the widest gap [S: 04-checks/check-claim-4.md §1 item 2]. On quality, Tunguz's footnote finds one significant result in 42 comparisons, fewer than chance alone would produce, and none that survives correction for running so many tests. He calls the quality gap "undemonstrated at this sample size, not proven absent" [S: 04-checks/check-claim-4.md §1 items 5 and 8]. An arXiv study from 8 September found no clear average effect of the harness on quality either [S: 04-checks/check-claim-4.md §2, How it shifted].

So the defensible claim is narrower than the version The ECHO Files' roundups ran in September [S: 01-the-monthly-september-2026.md, What I got wrong]. A harness can change what the same work costs several times over. Neither of September's two studies showed that it changes average quality, in either direction [S: 04-checks/check-claim-4.md §1 items 5 and 8; §2, How it shifted].

Savings by deletion

Where the wrapper saved money in production, part of the saving came from making it smaller [S: 01-the-monthly-september-2026.md#4, step 3]. On 23 September Cursor said harness changes had cut users' token costs by 7% without reducing agent quality, measured in A/B tests on a large user base [S: 04-checks/check-claim-4.md §2, How it shifted and Read these]. It trimmed roughly 66% of its system prompt, because long prompts slowed the agent down, and loaded tools only when they were needed, cutting description tokens in its static context by 60% [S: 04-checks/check-claim-4.md §2, How it shifted].

Dan McAteer had made the case in Latent Space on 28 August: harness progress is measured by how much of the harness gets deleted [S: 04-checks/check-claim-4.md §2, How it shifted]. He cited Anthropic cutting 80% of Claude Code's system prompt [S: 04-checks/check-claim-4.md §2, How it shifted]. On 29 September Anthropic's Thariq Shihipar said the core harness has to get more complex and more secure, and that he expects the Claude.md file to disappear eventually [S: 04-checks/check-claim-4.md §2, How it shifted]. On that view, what a harness sheds is instruction, and what it keeps is machinery: tools, security and limits.

The labs move in on routing

Routing, which had looked like the wrapper's best business, moved toward the labs [S: 01-the-monthly-september-2026.md#4, Verdict]. Jev, a model that only classifies, routes and scores, claimed more than 100 times the speed of small frontier models at more than 200 times lower cost, on its own figures, and six clones shipped in two days [S: 04-checks/check-claim-4.md §2, How it shifted]. OpenAI built its own, the Decisions API, in about a week, by Latent Space's account, and launched it in limited preview at DevDay on 29 September [S: 04-checks/check-claim-4.md §2, How it shifted; 04-checks/check-claim-1.md §2, How it shifted]. AWS and Cloudflare released decision models on 1 October [S: 04-checks/check-claim-3.md §2, How it shifted]. Days earlier, on 25 September, Latent Space had reported Stripe's purchase of OpenRouter at $7B, which put a price on a routing wrapper [S: 04-checks/check-claim-4.md §2, How it shifted].

Where the savings came from

Set the wrapper's numbers beside the market's. Ramp's AI index, reported by The Decoder on 2 October, showed businesses using more AI and paying less for it: usage up about 50% since spending peaked in July [S: 04-checks/check-claim-4.md §2, How it shifted]. The Decoder put nearly all of the fall in spending down to OpenAI and Anthropic competing [S: 04-checks/check-claim-4.md §2, How it shifted]. It doesn't mention harnesses or wrappers. This magazine's reading, and it is an inference, is that the savings businesses actually banked came from two labs competing rather than from anything their operators built.

When the model sets the bill

The strongest case against the argument is Databricks. It rolled Astra out to about 3,500 engineers, and coding spend rose 60% [S: 04-checks/check-claim-3.md §2, The best case against]. Gains were clear only on highly complex work, and AINews reported that simpler coding had not improved much [S: 04-checks/check-claim-3.md §2, The best case against; §1 item 4]. There the model set both the cost and the outcome [S: 01-the-monthly-september-2026.md#3, The best case against].

Databricks' answer was to put Astra on its own sub-budget [S: 04-checks/check-claim-3.md §2, The best case against]. Keep the expensive model for the work that needs it, and give its spending a line of its own.

The operator's tool

Put the month together and the operator's tool is a routing table: one row per job, with a default model, a trigger for escalating to a bigger one, a cost per accepted output and a spend cap [S: 01-the-monthly-september-2026.md#3, Why it matters and throughline 2]. To build one:

  1. Re-price your three most frequent AI jobs per accepted output, not per token. Run each on two $2/$10 models, such as Sonnet 5.5 and GPT-6.1 Sol, and log the tokens and the cost [S: 01-the-monthly-september-2026.md#3, throughline 1; 04-checks/check-claim-3.md §2, The throughline].
  2. Before the next model swap, keep the model and change the harness. Price one recurring workflow per completed task under yours and under a minimal one such as Pi [S: 01-the-monthly-september-2026.md#4, throughline 1].
  3. Do a deletion pass on one agent. Cut the instructions, guardrails and tool definitions the current model no longer needs, load tools only when they're used, and keep only what a rerun proves you need [S: 01-the-monthly-september-2026.md#4, throughline 2].
  4. Write the table: job, default model, escalation trigger. Put the $10/$50 frontier models on their own sub-budget, as Databricks did for Astra, and cap it [S: 01-the-monthly-september-2026.md#3, throughline 2; 04-checks/check-claim-3.md §2, The throughline].
  5. Move any call that only returns a label or a route to a cheap decision model [S: 01-the-monthly-september-2026.md#4, throughline 3].
  6. Build a rerunnable eval from 10 to 20 past outputs for each job, and set a hard monthly spend cap per job [S: 01-the-monthly-september-2026.md#3, throughline 3].

The eval is what keeps the table honest. A cheaper row is cheaper only if its output still gets accepted, and the month's main harness study couldn't rule a quality gap in or out [S: 04-checks/check-claim-4.md §1 item 5].

The cost of a task is the price of a token times the tokens the job burns, and the harness decides much of the second number. September moved both. Only the first is on the announcement.

How we know

The list prices come from Simon Willison's price table of 22 September, Anthropic's launch and pricing pages, and Zvi Mowshowitz's posts of 12 September and 1 October. The per-task figures for Astra and Argon come from AINews at Latent Space (4 September and 1 October), Databricks from AINews on 17 September, HarnessTax from Tom Tunguz's write-up and GIGAZINE's report (both 17 September), Cursor's savings from its own post (23 September), and Ramp's index from The Decoder (2 October) [S: 04-checks/check-claim-3.md §2; 04-checks/check-claim-4.md §2]. Separate checkers went line by line through the September monthly this piece draws on. On model choice they confirmed 35 lines and flagged 8 as wrong; on the wrapper, 37 confirmed, 8 wrong and one left unsettled that this piece doesn't use. This piece uses the corrected versions [S: 04-checks/check-claim-3.md §4; 04-checks/check-claim-4.md §4]. Still uncertain: HarnessTax's own page didn't load for the checkers, so its figures are confirmed only through Tunguz and GIGAZINE [S: 04-checks/check-claim-4.md §1, Note]. The AINews posts on Astra and Argon are paid and were checked as far as their public previews, and the Argon preview doesn't say how AINews worked out its per-task costs [NEW: https://www.latent.space/p/ainews-gemini-4-argon-gdms-answer]. Argon's discount has no announced end date, and most buyers can't use the model yet [S: 04-checks/check-claim-3.md §1 items 1 and 5]. Anthropic's 40% and 30%, and Jev's speed and cost figures, are the vendors' own. The AWS and Cloudflare releases rest on press coverage, and Claire Vo's detailed scores couldn't be checked [S: 04-checks/check-claim-3.md §1 item 6 and Also worth knowing].

Prove us wrong

The claim here most likely to be wrong is that a thinner harness cuts the cost of a task without cutting its quality. It rests on one study in which a quality gap is unproven at 42 comparisons rather than ruled out, an arXiv paper that found no clear average effect, and Cursor's A/B tests of its own product [S: 04-checks/check-claim-4.md §1 item 5; §2, How it shifted]. What would change our mind: a harness comparison large enough to detect a quality gap, or production data in which a deletion pass cut tokens and raised failure or rework rates.
