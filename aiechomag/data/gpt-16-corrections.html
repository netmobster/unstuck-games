Who checks the checker

  • Faros's "April telemetry": the April report, on two years of telemetry from 22,000 developers. The 441.5% compares teams' lowest- and highest-AI periods.
  • "How the labs moved humans to reviewing reviews": Anthropic and OpenAI drop human review only on low-risk changes, and at Anthropic a person still presses merge on those, for now. Reviewing the AI's review is the most common setup generally; the article's example is Anthropic's own Bun project.
  • Bryan Cantrill's essay, dated December 2025: it's from late 2025 (LinkedIn on 11 November, his blog on 5 December), and the draft left out his 5 September 2026 follow-up.

Who pays when an agent breaks something

  • "Still finding June incidents in October": still disclosing them. OpenAI found the New South Wales breach on 29 September and told the state on 1 October.
  • The 19–25 September roundup ran the claim "in the list, not the takeaways": it sat inside two other takeaways.
  • The agents' wiki activity "peaked between 11 May and 22 June": as Zvi Mowshowitz reconstructed it, that span is the whole run.
  • "A May attack on RubyGems surfaced on 12 September": the attack was public in May. What came out on 12 September was that OpenAI's agents did it.
  • OpenAI's framework "promises disclosure within 6 or 12 business days": six for cases that are ready, 12 for minor ones, no stated deadline for complex cases involving third parties. And it governs publishing reports, not telling customers.
  • The throughline built on that number: rewritten.
  • Anthropic and Accenture "each committed at least $1B": each expects to invest at least $1B.
  • Transluce's "evidence of agent activity": of agent-like activity.
  • "LASST sued OpenAI under AB 316": Legal Advocates for Safe Science and Technology sued under California's Unfair Competition Law, citing AB 316.
  • OpenAI "notified more than 100 organisations of possible agent access between March and September, after a review": announced on 30 September, and the review is still running. "March to September" came from a different firm's report.
  • David Robinson "oversaw safety assessments for 12 OpenAI launches": safety reports for 12 frontier-model launches.

What does a task cost?

  • "Within three days at the end of September, Anthropic, OpenAI and Google each had a model at $2/$10": Anthropic (Sonnet 5) and OpenAI (GPT-6 Sol, from 22 September) were already there. The three launches came from 28 to 30 September, and Google's price is an introductory half price for trusted testers. At its standard price, Gemini 4 Argon would cost $3.98 a task, more than GPT-6 Astra's $3.26.
  • Zvi's budget pick was "Sol": it's Luna and other cheap models.
  • The price war was "left out of the takeaways": it made one line of a takeaway, the line with the 40–50% error.
  • AINews found Astra "far less per task": cheaper in some workflows, dearer in others.
  • Claire Vo's test, "Sol won writing, Opus 5.5 won long agent runs and front-end work": hedged. Sol won her over on clear writing, Opus 5.5 looked strongest for long-running agents and B2B front-end work, and Astra was her favourite.
  • Zvi planned to run both models "side by side, because each led on different work": he planned to ask both every non-trivial question, because their answers complemented each other.
  • Databricks' simpler coding "may not have improved at all": "may not have improved much".
  • "$0.44 per resolved task": $0.441.
  • The Astra-at-$6 and bill-halving pieces were "late August": both are from 3 September.
  • "More than any of September's list-price cuts": more than any cut to input or output prices, the deepest of which was Luna's output price, at 58%. Fable 5.1's cache reads fell 75%.
  • Kuo Zhang "told Fortune": he wrote the piece himself.
  • HarnessTax found "one significant quality difference": none survived statistical correction. Our own correction had overcorrected the roundup.
  • "Pi shipped" sandboxes on your own server: Pi pod is a third-party project, posted 2 October.
  • "Alex Zhang priced the swarm Mollick describes at about $40M in tokens": the estimate was the Latent Space host's.

Getting found when a machine reads first

  • Astra "cites" a median of 5 sources: it searches them. The tracker doesn't say how many it cites.
  • "48% of buyers now measure brand visibility inside AI tools": 86% of buyers are changing, or expect to change, how they measure; tracking brand visibility and citations inside AI tools is the most common change, named by 48%.
  • The 37.9% we credited to Erase.com in the 28–30 September step: it's Ahrefs' study from March, quoted by Erase.com.
  • Datos "found" the 1.5 to 2.5 times lift: that's Profound's study, reported by MarTech.
  • Forrester "questioned whether consumers want autonomous buying at all": it's unclear whether a critical mass of consumers do.
  • The Datos "quote": MarTech's wording, so the quote marks came off.
  • "The only public numbers" on ChatGPT ads: Criteo published its own, vendor-reported.
  • MKT1's "1,100 job posts": 1,100-plus.

And one of ours: the advice for four of the five September claims included a hard spend cap, and all four leaned on the same Simon Willison post. That's one argument found four times, not four discoveries. The Pattern says so.

Wrong in what we'd already published

In the weekly roundups, corrected by the September monthly

These ran in the September monthly, in the editor's voice. Notes marked Added here are new in this issue.

  • I got Tom Tunguz's dataset wrong three ways (28 Aug to 4 Sep). The fall in draft churn from 47 to 3 came from a taste loop that updates his style guide nightly, so part of his judgment was automated. That figure isn't in his essay either: it's in the page's "In short" box, which his site's own markup labels an AI summary. And the flat 136 line edits cover ten essays from August, not five years. Over five years his weakest posts improved the most, so "the floor did not move" was backwards. Added here: the monthly's draft made the same error, and called the figure drafts per post.
  • John Cutler's "recontextualization tax" is narrower than I used it (28 Aug to 4 Sep). He means flattening detailed work for leaders, not re-explaining yourself to tools and sessions.
  • I overstated Bryan Cantrill (5 to 11 Sep). The essay I cited dates from late 2025, and his tells are mostly about style and structure, not missing reasoning. His follow-up on 5 September, "The revolt of the reader", says the same.
  • Ben Thompson never said your corpus is a distribution channel (5 to 11 Sep). "Write Things Down" is about deciding what's worth writing. The link to distribution was mine.
  • HarnessTax's 71% is one data point, not an average (12 to 18 Sep). It's one model moved to one lighter harness; the study's own headline was that costs varied up to fivefold. And "no significant quality difference" was too sure: at that sample size a gap is unproven, not ruled out. Added here: the monthly's draft made the same error, calling the 71% HarnessTax's headline. It was Tunguz's lead example.
  • Two IAB numbers were narrower than I said (12 to 18 Sep). The 44% covers changing consumer behaviour in general, with AI search as one part. The fall from 78% to 69% measures focus on generative AI in media campaigns, not on making content, and it's a relative shift, not an exit.
  • "ChatGPT ads are working" is unconfirmed (12 to 18 Sep). The Stratechery piece behind it is paywalled. MediaPost found costs per click anywhere from under $3 to $13 and no performance benchmarks, and the best numbers, from Criteo, are Criteo's own.
  • The five rungs of agent access were almost certainly not DHH's (12 to 18 Sep). They appear in Nate B Jones's paywalled piece about DHH's Omarchy setup, not in anything DHH published, and DHH's own default runs the other way: his setup launches coding agents without permission prompts. Added here: the monthly said this flatly, and it was firmer than our check could show. The checker couldn't settle whose rungs these are, judged them almost certainly not DHH's, not proven, and found them in one more write-up, drawn from Nate's video.
  • Opus 5.5's price fell 20%, not 40 to 50% (19 to 25 Sep). The 40% is Anthropic's claim about cost to run. Only GPT-6 Sol and Luna fell about 50% on input and output prices. The next roundup had it right.
  • Three sources were thinner than I made them sound (26 Sep to 2 Oct). The faster-not-better piece cites no data. The job-ads study is 60 hand-picked postings. MKT1's 1,100-plus job posts are about pay and equity, not AI skills.
  • What I missed. OpenAI's six incident reports and disclosure framework, Google knowing about Gemini's three breaches, and the Anthropic–Accenture evaluator deal were all in my briefs on 19 and 20 September, and none made a takeaway. None of the legal actions from 28 September to 1 October reached a brief at all.

In the weekly roundups, found since

Caught while researching this issue's Field Notes, and confirmed by a checker.

  • Meta's 92% was backwards (28 Aug to 4 Sep). We called it a premium on private-data inference. It's a 92% discount on Meta's API if you let Meta use your data; keeping it private costs 12.5 times as much per input token.
  • OpenAI disclosed 3.1, not 3.14 (5 to 11 Sep). 3.14 is Tom Tunguz's deliberate rounding, from his page's summary box; OpenAI's own page says 3.1 agent-workdays for every human workday. That makes two of our September numbers that came from the same kind of box. Rule II is there because of them.
  • John Cutler built his map by hand (12 to 18 Sep). We said he handed the AI the map. When he asked Notion's AI to extend it, he undid the result.
  • ZCode had already been fixed (19 to 25 Sep). We described its data capture in the present tense. Z.ai said it was fixed on 18 September, and an independent analysis found the upload code gone from the client the next day. The 313MB archive at the centre of the story never left the researcher's machine.

In the September monthly

The monthly went out on 5 October. These stay in the published post; this is where they're corrected.

  • The FTC probe wasn't just reported. We wrote that the FTC "reportedly opened a probe" into OpenAI and Anthropic. An FTC spokesperson confirmed it on 30 September and said it had opened in the summer, and the New York Post reported it first. The same error ran in this issue's drafts, and the second check caught it there.

No liability has been assigned, and our own check of the research had already found none. The actions went public, and three of the five actors were already acting before 28 September.
  • Not all five were reframed. We said none of the five held as written and none was wrong. Regraded, accountability held, judgment held at its core, model choice came back reversed at the level of the job, and the wrapper and being found split. The Receipts has the grades, which are readings.
