What does a task cost?

By the end of September the sticker price no longer told the big labs apart, and on the magazine's reading the number that did was the cost of a finished task.

Feature // 10 min read

In early September, a weekly roundup from The ECHO Files said model choice had become the least consequential decision in the stack. By October, on the magazine's reading, the evidence had split that claim, and one half had come back reversed. The vendor mattered less. Which model got which job mattered more, and so did what was wrapped around it. The number worth counting was the cost of a finished task, rather than the price of a token, and the same roundup had already named what you spend per task among the decisions that do count.

The sticker converged

Some prices were falling before the month began. OpenAI cut Luna's price 80% in July, while Anthropic held Opus at $5/$25 per million input and output tokens across five releases.

Then came the price war. On 22 September Anthropic released Claude Opus 5.5 at $4/$20, a 20% cut. About an hour later OpenAI released GPT-6 Sol at $2/$10, half GPT-5.6 Sol's promotional $4/$20, and GPT-6 Luna at $0.10/$0.50. Between 28 and 30 September, Anthropic, OpenAI and Google each launched or announced a model at $2/$10: Claude Sonnet 5.5, GPT-6.1 Sol and Gemini 4 Argon.

Two of the three labs were already there. Sonnet 5 had sold at $2/$10 since its launch, and Anthropic's pricing page says a planned rise to $3/$15 won't happen. GPT-6 Sol had been at $2/$10 for a week. Google's $2/$10 is an introductory half price.

So the sticker stopped separating vendors, and Anthropic began selling in other units. Anthropic's launch page said Opus 5.5 costs 40% less to run than Opus 5 on typical workloads. That is a claim about work rather than tokens, and it is Anthropic's own; the list price fell 20%. Sonnet 5.5 kept its list price, and Anthropic says it costs up to 30% less per task because it needs fewer tokens.

Argon's three prices

Gemini 4 Argon shows what a token price hides. It has three prices.

The first is on the announcement: $2/$10, a 50% introductory discount with no end date announced. It is open first to government users, Google staff and trusted cyber defenders in Google's Fairwind Program. Developers, enterprises and consumers come later.

The second is the standard price behind the discount: $4/$20.

The third is the one a bill is made of. Argon matched GPT-6 Astra at 53 on Artificial Analysis's Intelligence Index, but averaged 62,000 output tokens per task to Astra's 27,000. Astra lists at $10/$50, so Argon's introductory price is a fifth of Astra's per token. AINews put the cost at $1.99 a task against Astra's $3.26; output tokens make up under half of each. At its standard price, Argon would come to $3.98 a task, more than Astra.

Per job from the start

The month's first evidence was already priced by the hour and by the task. On 3 September Latent Space put GPT-6 Astra at under $6 an hour: 33 tokens a second at a top rate of $50 per million tokens. The next day AINews found Astra 2.5 times pricier per token than its predecessor, cheaper per task in some workflows and dearer in others (a paid post, checked as far as its public preview).

The comparisons that followed split by job. On 10 September Nate B Jones compared Claude Fable 5.1 and Astra under the title "Which Model Gets Which Job", and concluded that one project could give him reasons to use both (a paid post, checked as far as its free opening). Zvi Mowshowitz planned to put every non-trivial question to both models for a while, because their answers to one hard question had complemented each other. On 22 September Claire Vo's blind test on How I AI split three ways. GPT-6 Sol won her over on clear writing, Opus 5.5 looked strongest for long-running agents and B2B front-end work, and Astra was her favourite.

On 26 September Zvi published a job map: Opus 5.5 by default, Fable 5.1 for correctness in expert domains, Astra for ambitious coding and some science work, and Luna and other cheap models for budget work. That is a routing table, written as a blog post. By 3 October Jones was routing in practice, keeping Astra for the most demanding work and moving recurring jobs to Sol to save his plan allowance.

The wrapper's number, and its limits

The other lever on the bill is the wrapper. Alibaba.com's president, Kuo Zhang, wrote in Fortune on 9 September that work gets done by three things together: the model; a harness that equips it with tools and memory and lets it act; and context on what good looks like in a given industry.

On 17 September Tom Tunguz wrote up HarnessTax, a study that ran the same models under different harnesses. His lead example was one switch. GPT-5.6 Sol moved from Claude Code to Pi, a minimal open-source harness, and its cost per resolved task fell from $1.540 to $0.441, a 71% cut. That is larger than any September cut to input or output token prices. Fable 5.1's cache reads did fall 75% at the start of the month, so the comparison holds for input and output prices only.

The limits matter as much as the number. The 71% is one model moved to one harness, not an average. HarnessTax's own headline, as GIGAZINE reported it, was that success rates didn't vary significantly while costs differed by up to five times, so the 71% isn't even the widest gap. On quality, Tunguz's footnote finds one significant result in 42 comparisons, fewer than chance alone would produce, and none that survives correction for running so many tests. He calls the quality gap "undemonstrated at this sample size, not proven absent". An arXiv study from 8 September found no clear average effect of the harness on quality either.

So the defensible claim is narrower than the version The ECHO Files' roundups ran in September. A harness can change what the same work costs several times over. Neither of September's two studies showed that it changes average quality, in either direction.

Savings by deletion

Where the wrapper saved money in production, part of the saving came from making it smaller. On 23 September Cursor said harness changes had cut users' token costs by 7% without reducing agent quality, measured in A/B tests on a large user base. It trimmed roughly 66% of its system prompt, because newer models needed less direction, and loaded tools only when they were needed, cutting description tokens in its static context by 60%.

Dan McAteer had made the case in Latent Space on 22 August: harness progress is measured by how much of the harness gets deleted. He cited Anthropic cutting 80% of Claude Code's system prompt. On 29 September Anthropic's Thariq Shihipar said the core harness has to get more complex and more secure, and that he expects the Claude.md file to disappear eventually. On the magazine's reading, what a harness sheds is instruction, and what it keeps is machinery: tools, security and limits.

A lab moves in on routing

Routing, which had looked like the wrapper's best business, drew in a lab and two cloud platforms. Jev, a model that only classifies, routes and scores, claimed more than 100 times the speed of small frontier models at more than 200 times lower cost, on its own figures, and six clones shipped in two days. OpenAI built its own, the Decisions API, in about a week, by Latent Space's account, and launched it in limited preview at DevDay on 29 September. AWS and Cloudflare released decision models on 1 October. Days earlier, on 25 September, Latent Space had reported Stripe's purchase of OpenRouter at $7B, which put a price on a routing wrapper.

Where the savings came from

Set the wrapper's numbers beside the market's. Ramp's AI index, reported by The Decoder on 2 October, showed businesses using more AI and paying less for it: usage up about 50% since spending peaked in July. The Decoder put nearly all of the fall in spending down to OpenAI and Anthropic competing. It doesn't mention harnesses or wrappers. This magazine's reading, and it is an inference, is that the savings businesses actually banked came from two labs competing rather than from anything their operators built.

When the model sets the bill

The strongest case against the argument is Databricks. It rolled Astra out to about 3,500 engineers, and coding spend rose 60%. Gains were clear only on highly complex work, and AINews reported that simpler coding may not have improved much. On the monthly's reading, the model set both the cost and the outcome there.

Databricks' answer was to put Astra on its own sub-budget. The magazine's lesson: keep the expensive model for the work that needs it, and give its spending a line of its own.

The operator's tool

Put the month together and, on the magazine's reading, the operator's tool is a routing table: one row per job, with a default model, a trigger for escalating to a bigger one, a cost per accepted output and a spend cap. The Operator's guides to model routing and spend caps, in 12, set out how to build one.

Prove us wrong

The claim here most likely to be wrong is that a thinner harness cuts the cost of a task without cutting its quality. It rests on one study in which a quality gap is unproven at 42 comparisons rather than ruled out, an arXiv paper that found no clear average effect, and Cursor's A/B tests of its own product. What would change our mind: a harness comparison large enough to detect a quality gap, or production data in which a deletion pass cut tokens and raised failure or rework rates.
