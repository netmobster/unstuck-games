The Parlor

How it holds

Most story games forget who you are by the second evening. This one can’t.

A chat log drifts. Elsible doesn’t run on one. Your life is written down before you take a step in it, and your narrator keeps the record while you play.

I

The life is written first

Your cards become a document before the first scene: who you are, where this is, who’s at the centre, who’s around you, how it’s told. The story grows from that, not from whatever was said last.

II

Your narrator keeps the record

Every person you meet, every promise, every door you open stays on the record. Weeks later, the one who tested you remembers what you answered.

III

The half you don’t get told is already moving

A secret, a twist, what happens if nobody intervenes. It’s written as you step through the door, so the story is going somewhere whether you push or not.

Here’s the thing about being a bridesmaid for the fourth time: you know exactly where the bar is before the venue does. So when the groom’s brother, who has said eleven words to you in two years and nine of them were “excuse me”, sits down on the stool next to yours and says “I need a favour,” you know two things. One, this is the rehearsal dinner and your ex is at table six with someone who does Pilates. Two, you already know what the favour is.

“My mother,” he says, to his drink, “thinks I’m bringing someone.”

You say: “You’re kidding. Who?”

He doesn’t answer, which, you’ll realise later, was the answer. The group chat has already noticed where you’re sitting. It has opinions. One of them is a photo.

You can turn the card, but it costs you.

When your life is set, the reader plays one card, face down. Leave it and open the door, and you get the life you picked. Turn it, and the doorkeeper sees what you turned and judges you for it: now and then a gift, mostly a price. You’re told which way up it landed. Never what it changed.

The doorkeeper

Where anything else... is possible.

The reader only deals. Your narrator only tells. In between, the doorkeeper opens your envelope, judges her card if you turned it, and writes the life you’re about to live, out of sight. That’s why nothing in your envelope is secret: the secrets don’t exist until the door.

I

You hand it over

Your envelope: the cards you kept, and her card if you turned it.

II

Her card is judged

If you turned it. You’re told upright or reversed, and nothing else. Upright is a gift. Reversed is a price, and you’ll spend the story looking for it.

III

Your life is written

Out of sight, random cards become one real person: a name, a past, what they want, the people around them. It’s read back and checked until every piece holds, and the half you don’t get told is set underneath.

IV

The door opens

You’re shown the life you’re stepping into, and one line into your first morning. From there the story belongs to your narrator, and to what you do.
