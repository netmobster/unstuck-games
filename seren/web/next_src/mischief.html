I · A few from the deck Coming in 1.5

She never explains.

The Roll That Means Nothing

She asks for a d20, nods gravely at the result, writes something down, and moves on. Not a word.

How Much Do You Weigh?

Out of nowhere, she asks exactly how much your character weighs. Writes it down. Moves on.

The Goose Has a Grudge

One goose. It follows you through town and honks at the worst possible moment, every time.

The Unexplained Cow

A cow is standing in the road. No spell, tracker or interrogation will ever say why.

The Sword With a Sense of Humour

A talking blade that only works when it finds the situation funny. It has opinions about comedy.

The Pickle Dependency

A merchant’s pickles give you a full night’s rest. You come to rely on them. He comes back with plain ones, at a premium.

Haunted House Wants an Exorcist

A mimic disguised as a cottage hires you to clear the ghosts out of it. From the inside.

The Mystery Box Skips Fourteen Months

A wild effect throws you a year into the future mid-fight. Every villain kept working while you were gone.

#118The Wand Keeps Receipts

A cursed wand makes one enemy a day vanish. You come to rely on it. A year later, she rolls to see how many come back.

Collected from real tables and retold. #118 is Jay’s own. Each one is scored for whether it adds to what’s happening; the ones that only distract stay in the box.

III · How it runs

Mischief with honest bookkeeping.

The Director picks, in code.

Seeded dice read the campaign’s log and its villains’ clocks. No model decides when mischief happens, so it can’t be talked into it or out of it.

The narrator tells it without knowing why.

She gets what to do, never the reason. A mystery stays a mystery to the storyteller too.

One question before anything fires.

An outside judge asks: does this add to what’s happening, or distract from it? It can only say no.

A seven-turn floor.

Nothing fires within seven turns of the last surprise, whatever the judge says. Randomness that entertains, not randomness that has you chasing side quests.

A ceiling on chaos.

About three threads at once, never more than six. At the ceiling it merges or revives an old thread, and never opens a new one.

Long cons wait in the cold pile.

The wand’s receipts sit quietly until their date comes, and only count when they fire.

Every trick is scored.

Funny, story, solo fit and cost, 1 to 3 each. Agents score all of them, Jay vetoes, and the best ship.

Each campaign has allow and block lists.

Creators decide which tricks can ever reach their table. Cruel is off by default.

IV · Not a random encounter table

A random encounter interrupts the story. Mischief is part of it.

  1. It makes the world feel watched.

    A DM who asks your weight and says nothing has told you something is coming. The tension is the content.

  2. It rewards paying attention.

    The goose comes back. The pickle merchant comes back. The wand’s bill comes due. Callbacks are what make a table feel like it remembers you.

  3. It’s character, not noise.

    Each DM persona carries its own list: the Jester’s mischief isn’t the Rulemaker’s. You learn who’s telling the story by what they get up to.

  4. It’s fair.

    Dice the model can’t fudge, a log you can read, a judge that can only veto, and a floor that keeps it rare. Mischief never quietly changes an outcome.

  5. It pays off at the end.

    The chronicle lists what was dealt and never drawn, and what was still waiting in the cold pile. You find out what the DM had planned.

Random encounters fill time. Mischief makes you look over your shoulder, and then tells you, at the end, what was behind you.
