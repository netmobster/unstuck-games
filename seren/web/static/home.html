The difference

SEREN doesn't execute a campaign. It runs one.

You give it the bones — structure, beats, characters, factions, your own items and spells and rules. What happens between those bones is improvised at the table, by a DM that knows the world. Which means the same material never plays out the same way twice.

The rest of the party

They can disagree with you, and sometimes they're right.

The hard part of playing alone is not the world. It is that nobody at the table surprises you. SEREN runs the two or three companions beside you as people, not as options — they want things, they have opinions about what you are doing, and they act on both.

01

They do things you didn't ask for.

A companion can go ahead without you, hold something back, or settle a problem in a way you would not have chosen. You find out afterwards, like anyone at a table would.

02

They are not a menu.

You cannot pick their next line or spend them like a resource. They carry their own wants, and those wants do not always agree with yours.

03

They remember how you treated them.

Trust is earned and spent. What you did in session four is still what you did, and the person who watched you do it has not forgotten.

Off script

No plan survives contact with the player.

Going off script isn't a failure mode of tabletop. It is the whole appeal. You will always do the thing nobody prepared for — and a system that only knows the plan treats that as an error. SEREN takes the detour as canon and keeps building from there.

How it works

Three things, deliberately kept apart.

Most tools fuse the story, the rules and the current situation into one long conversation. SEREN separates them. That separation is the whole architecture — and the reason the system isn't bound to any single game.

I

World

Canon, characters, places, factions, history, secrets, the things that are simply true. Imported, or grown at the table.

II

Ruleset

D&D 5e, with house rules treated as canon. A ruleset is a layer, not the foundation — which is why it can be swapped.

III

State

Every change the party caused, who witnessed it, what it cost, and what it set in motion. Live, and carried forward.

D&D is where SEREN starts. It isn't where SEREN ends. Because the world, the rules and the live state are separate concerns, the architecture doesn't care which game you're playing.

Consequence

A death in session four is still a death in session forty.

One decision at the table doesn't resolve. It propagates — through people, places, factions and time — and the world goes on acting on it while nobody is looking.

Run it twice

The same bones. A different campaign.

Three anchors, written once. One person, playing them twice, months apart. Nothing below was authored — and both playthroughs are faithful to what was handed over.

AA rival wants the tower at Shinebright. BSer'en carries a debt she cannot pay. CThe Ashgate compact breaks before winter.
Playthrough one14 sessions
  1. AThe rival is bought off rather than killed. He becomes a landlord, and a problem.
  2. BThe party clears Ser'en's debt for her. Now she owes them, and resents it.
  3. CThe compact breaks in a council chamber, over a signature.
Winter arrives and there is no war. There is a lawsuit.
Playthrough twoSame bones · 9 sessions
  1. AThe rival reaches the tower first. Four sessions are spent getting it back.
  2. BThe debt is called in publicly. Ser'en leaves the party for six weeks to work it off.
  3. CThe compact breaks over a body nobody meant to leave.
Winter arrives mid-siege, and the tower is the only shelter for miles.

Every anchor was honoured. Neither campaign was written. This is what a DM does, and it is what a script cannot.

Who owns what

Nobody overrules the dice. Not the model, and not you.

The split isn't judgement against bookkeeping. It is what can be known exactly against what has to be inferred — and one column that belongs to nobody.

Seren owns
  • Narration
  • NPC voice
  • Adjudication
  • Pacing
  • What a moment means
  • What it costs
  • What it reveals

Everything that has to be inferred in the moment. This is the DM's work, and SEREN does it.

Nobody owns
  • What is true
  • The dice
  • The result

A roll can be interpreted. It cannot be reversed. One test: did the success or failure state change? If it did, someone cheated.

You own
  • Your character
  • Your choices
  • What you do about the consequences

One character, played properly, in a world that will not bend to make you comfortable.

For creators

You already built it. Nobody ever played it.

Most campaigns don't die from bad ideas. They die from calendars. The world got finished, the group didn't survive the year, and the folder has been sitting there ever since — a bible, a map, forty pages of notes, and no way to find out whether any of it works.

SEREN reads what you already made. Hand it the bible, the notes, the wiki, the half-written sourcebook. It works out what things are called, what is true in your world, and what you have and haven't decided — and then it asks you the questions you never had to answer, because you never got to the table.

Then you play it. Not pitch it, not finish it. Play it, this week, as one character, and find out which parts land. The players you could never get to a table are the ones sitting beside you.
