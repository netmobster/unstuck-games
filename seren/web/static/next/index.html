Serenista’s book · Claude Opus 5.5 · 26 Nov

Blood and Ink — your book

You are Serenista, and the woman who took you in is dead. Two mornings ago Agnes Tull was found at the bottom of her own pressing pit with her head broken on the stone. The whole county has agreed she fell in drink, though in eleven years you never once saw her drink. Tonight the five families come to her house for the wake, and at noon tomorrow she goes into the ground with whatever she knew.

You are standing in Agnes’s house beside the press at Thornby, in the Sallow. It is dusk, the candles are lit, and the first guests are already through the door.

You

Serenista, Druid. A Saltgate street child grown into the keeper’s right hand, who still listens to dogs more readily than to people.

  • Where you came from. Saltgate: the gutters, the quays, the cold. You were a street rat there, and you would have stayed one.
  • Who took you in. Agnes took you in off those streets eleven years ago, though she did not have to. She paid for you somehow, and she never told you what it cost or who she paid. You have lived beside the press ever since, learning fruit and weather and her ledger.
  • What you suspect about yourself. Somebody paid to get you out of Saltgate, and people who pay are usually patient. You have always half-believed the debt was never settled, only carried, and that Agnes carried it for you. Now she is gone, and you do not know who holds it.
  • What you know. Agnes Tull never drank, not once in eleven years.
  • What you suspect. Somebody was in the press house after Agnes fell. Her lantern is back on its hook, and it still has oil in it.

What you can do, in plain words

Speak with beastsFor a while you can talk with animals and understand them. They tell you what they felt and saw in their own terms, which are not ours.
Take a beast’s shapeTwice before you rest you can become a small, ordinary animal you have seen, such as a cat, a rat, or a dog, and go where a person would be noticed.
Mend and sootheYou can close wounds with a word or a touch, and berries you have blessed will feed and heal whoever eats them.
Find what is lostYou can sense the direction of a particular object you know, if it is near.
Nudge the green worldSmall natural workings: snuff a candle, foretell tomorrow’s weather, make a bud open, leave a scent on the air. You can also lend someone a little luck when they try something hard.
The light in youSomething old and bright runs in your blood. You see in the dark, you shrug off the touch of rot and shadow, and once a day you can heal with your hands alone.

Your people

Hal Pennock. He is the parson’s new curate, and this is his first burial. He is very keen to help, has offered to see to the arrangements, and knows parish procedure by heart, chapter and verse. He keeps the parish book, or at least he can get at it. If you ask him, he can tell you when any entry in it was written. He is earnest and quick to blush, and he holds his ledger like a shield.

Ha’penny. He has been your dog since the Saltgate days, and he has been the press dog for eleven years. He sleeps in the press house, so he would have been there that night. He has not settled since the morning they found her. He paces, he whines at doors, and he watches the crowd. He is the one creature in the Sallow you would trust with your life.

The Sallow, and how it works

  • Families. Five families hold the Sallow, and they have married each other for two hundred years. Every cousin is somebody’s in-law, and every quarrel is a family quarrel. You belong to none of them. That makes you free, and it also makes you nobody.
  • Money. Here money is fruit, and what fruit owes. Every orchard in the county brings its apples to the press at Thornby, because it is the only press no family owns. With the apples they bring their debts, and those go into Agnes’s ledger. Whoever holds the ledger knows who owes whom.
  • Law. Law in the Sallow is mostly the parish. Births, marriages, deaths, and burials are written in the parish book, and what the book says becomes what happened. The county officers come when sent for, and nobody sends for them.
  • Violence. Violence is scandal. A raised hand at a wake would be talked about for a generation, and the one who raised it would be finished here. People in the Sallow win with words, marriages, delays, and signatures.
  • Saltgate. Saltgate is the city, a long day away. Sallow folk speak of it the way you speak of a wound.

Tonight

The county has decided how Agnes died, and by noon tomorrow it will be written down and buried with her. Between now and then, everyone who might know otherwise is in one house, drinking her cider and saying kind things over her body.

Ha’penny will not come inside. Hal is already at your elbow with his ledger. Someone hung her lantern back on its hook, and you have until noon.

Agnes lies in her own parlour in a high-collared dress with long sleeves, candles at her head, and the room is already full of people who are all related to each other. Out in the yard Ha’penny will not come past the door; he stands on the step with his hackles up and his eyes on the crowd. A young man in a curate’s collar pushes through with a ledger open against his chest and a pencil behind his ear. He asks you, very politely, how "Tull" is spelled for the burial at noon, and whether "fell, in drink" is how the family would like it worded.

How this table plays

  • Who tells it. The dungeon master tells this story as someone who checks every claim against the ledger. You will get cross-references and footnotes you did not ask for, and no guesses. If something is said, it can be checked.
  • Dice. You roll only when the outcome is genuinely uncertain. If you ask a question that your character would simply know the answer to, you are told.
  • The stakes. This is about Agnes, about you, and about the truth of one death in one small county. The world will not end. What can be lost is her name, your place here, and whatever she paid for you.
  • The danger. Danger here is real. The Sallow settles things quietly, and violence is scandal, so it comes at night, in the dark, in places like the pit and the cellars, and it hurts when it comes.
  • Death. Your character can die. You will see it coming, and you will usually have a chance to step back. If you keep going down into the dark, it can happen.
  • Everything in this book is true. The rest is there to find.
