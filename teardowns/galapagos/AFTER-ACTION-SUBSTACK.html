What Came Out of the Ground

We dug up Galápagos: Mendel's Escape. A ferret climbed out.

Unstuck Games · After-action · Game #3. Tombstone: Galápagos: Mendel's Escape (1997). Shipped as Ferret Bowling: Bowling Optional Edition, alpha, 16 September 2026. Play it at ferretbowling.unstuck-games.com.

This is the companion to The Mendel File, the teardown that started it. That piece was the promise. This one is what survived contact.

The promise

Mendel's organ was simple and strange: you do not control the creature. You change the world it is in, and it responds in its own time.

Galápagos gave its creature a will and gave the player only the environment. You rearranged the room, and Mendel walked through it, occasionally into something fatal. The game was buried by its platform and its difficulty, but the organ was intact.

Two days, by the clock

  • 0:55 of Jay's time to the first playtest.
  • 24,000 simulated runs before he saw it.
  • 6 automated play styles, every invariant passing.
  • 10 item combos, every one of them firing.

The method's promise is that each game costs less of Jay's time than the last. Game #2 reached a rough prototype at about an hour and forty-five minutes. Game #3 reached a playtest at fifty-five, on a bigger idea, because the library had grown: the deterministic sim came from Orbis, the testing split came from Bad Monkeys, and the ledger thinking came from Elsewhere.

She has a fucking hoard.
— Jay, first playtest, run 2, minute 55

That reaction is the design validated in five words, and it arrived before any art, sound or polish existed. The hoard was not in the brief. It emerged because stolen things persisted between runs, and the sim had nowhere to put them but under the couch.

What we kept

  • The organ itself. You never steer Lucy. You pack a cage, open a door, and get out of the way. Every verb you have is a condition, not a command.
  • The world as the interface. Mendel's rooms became a seven-room house with real doors, real objects and real routes. The house is the control panel.
  • Consequence without punishment. Nothing kills Lucy. The cost of a bad idea is a wasted day and a grumpy ferret, which turned out to be enough.

What we cut

  • Difficulty as the teacher. Galápagos taught by killing you. Jay's boundary, "more adorable and weird than chaos and danger", removed death entirely, and the game got more interesting, not less.
  • The puzzle. Mendel is a puzzle game with an autonomous piece. Ferret Bowling has no solution and refuses a score. The payoff is the sentence at the end of the run.
  • The launcher. The genre promised throwing an animal for distance. Lucy cannot be aimed. The title kept the joke.

Four things nobody designed

  1. Moods are attention, not stats. The items you pack do not make Lucy stronger. They change what she notices. The same house produces a different day because she is looking at different things.
  2. The hoard is the retention hook. Treasures persist under the couch between runs. Nothing else in the game makes a player say "one more run".
  3. Habits make a specific ferret. A favourite nap spot, a favourite room, a nemesis, a routine, each fading if unused. When a mood meets a habit you get a ritual: the shoe nest, the snack sock. Persistence is what turns a simulation into a character.
  4. Leftover mood. Deciding to play with her does not mean she is in the mood for it. A slice of yesterday's temper survives into today, and a bath washes it away. That one rule stopped runs feeling independent of each other.

None of those four were in the brief. All four came out of testing.

The day after the alpha

The first version of this piece listed what was still wrong. Most of that list is gone, in one day, and each fix taught us something.

She has a voice now

The alpha shipped silent. Now the house plays Nocturnal Drift, a slow ambient piece Jay made for it, and Lucy dooks.

Her voice is one seventeen-second recording of a real ferret playing, with eighty-two separate calls inside it. Picking random moments out of that file would cut a dook in half about half the time, so we did the listening once, offline, and cut it into seventeen whole phrases, sorted by how worked up she was on the tape.

Then the important part: the sim decides when she makes a noise, not the speakers. Every action rolls on its own dice, weighted by what she is doing and what mood she is in. So her voice is part of the run's record. The same seed replays the same noises. On zoomies she makes a noise about most of what she does, and mostly loud ones. After a whispered insult she does not make a single sound.

Inspect her hoard

The hoard was a list of names. Now the end-of-run card turns over, and every object she has ever stolen is there, oldest first, with what she thought about it.

Shoe is nice. My shoe.
Am fish. Good fish.
Soft and warm. Soft and warm.

Those three lines are Jay's, and they set the voice for all of it: two tiny sentences, the second one either claims the thing or says the first one again. The thoughts are never invented. At the moment she steals something, the sim writes down the facts: what caught her eye, why she went for it, the mood she was in, and later, what she did with it. The words only report. Things the house tidied away stay on the list, crossed out, because she remembers what you took off her.

Some clipping issues on the hoard viewer, but sooooo cool.
— Jay, first look

The eye

A small eye on the hoard shows the working: the world seed, how many decisions she made, her last decision and the three things she nearly did instead with their real odds, and a replay string. It exists to prove a point. There is no AI in here. It is seeded dice and tag weights, and a curious player can reconstruct the run exactly. It only appears after the door closes, so it can never be used to steer one.

She carries what she steals

In the alpha, stolen things vanished the moment she grabbed them and reappeared as a line of text. The sim always knew she was carrying them; nothing drew it. Now they ride in her mouth all the way to the couch. The kind of bug a player notices long before a developer does.

She was proud 77% of the time

This one is worth being honest about. Jay asked whether she was always proud, because he only remembered seeing proud. We measured it across three thousand runs. He was right: seventy-seven per cent.

The end-of-run portrait checked "proud" first, and pride fired on any new habit or any combo, including ones you had already found. Her real moods, wired, dozy, grudgy, only showed on the rare days nothing new happened. Now pride is for firsts, and her actual mood outranks a new habit. A player's "I only remember seeing one face" was a better test than the test suite.

A story to start on

The first thing a new player saw was an alpha notice about bugs. Now it is Lucy, asleep in her cage, and a page that says she is about to have a very big day. You can't steer her. Nobody can steer her. Pack the cage, open the door, and get out of the way.

What is still wrong with it

  • Two sound moments are still missing: a game start and a run end. The room and the ferret are done; the punctuation is not.
  • Labels still collide in the living room when she drags things around.
  • The journal repeats itself sooner than it should. This is where pre-generated variety earns its place.
  • The art is placeholder quality: good enough for an alpha, not for a release.
  • Mobile is untested for this game specifically.

All of that is in the notice in small print, which says the quiet part: some of it is a bug, some of it is Lucy, and telling them apart is genuinely hard.

What game #3 adds to the library

  • Mood as attention: preparation changes what an agent notices, not what it can do.
  • Persistence that characterises: a stash, habits that fade, rituals where mood meets habit.
  • Leftover state across runs: a slice of yesterday survives, and one action can wash it away.
  • A voice on the dice: sound chosen by the sim's own seeded rolls, so it replays with the run.
  • Derived inner voice: a character's thoughts built only from facts the simulation recorded.
  • Show the working: the maths, available after the fact, as proof rather than dashboard.

Three games in, one verb

Orbis: conditions, never interference. Elsewhere: your absence is the mechanic. Bad Monkeys: she works while you are gone. Ferret Bowling: you cannot aim the ferret. Four games, one verb. You do not control the thing. You set up conditions and find out.

That is either an identity or a rut, and naming it is what chose game #4. The next tombstone is Majesty (2000), whose organ is the same refusal seen from the other side: you cannot give an order, only an offer, and the offer can be declined. The difference is that this time you are present, paying, and out of time.

The ferret taught us that persistence makes a character. The next one has to prove that twelve of them can.
