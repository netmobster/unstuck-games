SPRITES, NOT FADES

In the run she is one image — five rear-view poses in a strip, stepped hard with steps(5). A cross-fade between poses reads as a ghost; a hard swap reads as a scoot. The strip was re-cut into five equal cells because the source sheet's poses weren't evenly spaced and got sliced mid-animal.

WHAT CARRIES SPEED

Seams
Eight bars out of the vanishing point, widening as they arrive. The only true speed cue.
Stacks
Three lanes, staggered, scaling .12 → 1.5 and flipping as they pass her.
Lucy
Sways and bobs in screen space, never scales — the camera holds 2.4 m behind.
Route
Dashes march away from camera, so the wobble she already made stays readable.

TWO LUCYS, ON PURPOSE

On the page she faces you and does all the acting. In the hallway you only ever see her from behind, which hides the face so she never has to emote mid-run and lets the tail carry the pose. Fades are reserved for depth: the haze at the vanishing point, and stacks easing in as they leave it.

Current truth

Ferret Bowling stars Lucy, a real ferret. She lives in a cage. The player does not throw her, steer her, or directly control her. The player gets a short preparation phase, then opens the cage. Lucy decides what happens next. It is a launcher game whose payload is an autonomous animal.

You don't aim Lucy. You prepare Lucy.

The loop the player should feel, over and over: “I wonder what happens if I do this to Lucy…” → open door → lose control → watch her do something unexpected → see what she hits and picks up → learn something about her behaviour → spend the result on a longer, weirder run → try something else.

1 · Prep the cage

A · LUCY

Affect her mood. Snacks → zoomies. A wound-up sock → curiosity. A whispered insult → stubbornness. Purple salmon → extreme, unpredictable bouncing and wall ricochets. Future foods can alter speed, curiosity, attention, traction, wandering.

These are behavioural modifiers, not stat upgrades. Numbers may exist underneath. The player should experience “purple salmon makes Lucy fucking insane”, never “purple salmon gives +14% lateral velocity”.

B · SURFACE

Kitchen linoleum (fast, true) · hall floorboards (grippy, sudden) · long runner rug (slow, she burrows) · Woodbine Mall (polished, long, chaotic). Surfaces are different behavioural environments, not better/worse tiers.

2 · Open the door

This is the important moment. Control ends. Lucy runs autonomously, camera behind her. No steering, no move-left/right, no fake direct control. The player watches the consequences of the preparation.

3 · The run

It should feel physical despite being 2D. Rear-view sprites, hard stepped pose changes, scale and perspective, rotation and tilt, shadow movement, lane position, hallway and camera movement. No actual 3D needed. The five rear-view poses are the run animation source.

Lucy is expressive before the run; Lucy is physical during the run. Face for character, tail and body for motion. Do not merge those two jobs.

4 · Things in the hallway

The player does not choose these. They are simply there: toilet-paper rolls, slippers, rugs, socks, lamps, hats, cats, increasingly ridiculous objects. Established examples are on the page above, with their consequences.

The player doesn't build the chaos. The player creates the conditions in which Lucy creates the chaos.

5 · Score and progression

The progression resource is rolls. Lucy knocks things over → the player earns rolls → rolls buy longer and weirder hallways. The hallway is the progression. Lucy is not. She is never a consumable resource.

SHE ALWAYS COMES BACK. THAT IS NOT A MECHANIC. IT IS JUST TRUE.

Do not add lives, health, death, resurrection or veterinary costs.

6 · Progression must change the game

New surfaces introduce new physical behaviours — faster, grippier, burrowing, bouncing, obstacles, things she investigates, environmental reactions, increasingly ridiculous spaces. The player should unlock the next hallway because “I wonder what Lucy will do THERE”, not because it pays +20%.

7 · God Mode

Free is the whole game. God Mode is optional intervention and toys: one ad = one hour. It can preserve one pickup, deliberately introduce weird objects, apply extreme behavioural modifiers, and later sillier interventions. It must never be necessary to enjoy the game.

The money section is frozen

Do not rewrite, reorder, soften or improve any copy in #money. The you-get / never hierarchy, “No premium sock”, “A ferret behind a paywall” and “SHE DOES NOT KNOW WHAT A SUBSCRIPTION IS” are studio philosophy expressed through a ferret. Ship them exactly as written.

What is actually worth protecting

Indirect agency. The player decides before the run, then relinquishes control: prepare → release → observe → understand → try again. If the player starts steering Lucy directly, the central idea is dead. If every prep option reduces to “more speed / more distance / more points”, it is equally dead.

The goal is not optimal Lucy. The goal is learning Lucy — the player building a mental model: “Lucy gets distracted by this.” “Lucy hates that floor.” “Purple salmon was a terrible idea.” “Oh my god, she does THAT when she's curious.”

The game succeeds when the player feels they are experimenting with a creature whose behaviour they can influence but never fully control.

THE VISUAL AND SYSTEM LANGUAGE SHOULD REINFORCE

  • Lucy is already perfect. The player changes conditions.
  • The environment participates; the hallway creates surprises.
  • The run is observed, not piloted.
  • Numbers support the fantasy rather than replacing it.
  • Physical absurdity beats optimisation.
  • A weird outcome can be valuable even when it scores badly.
The player should create the conditions for the joke, not directly perform the joke.

Red-team test before adding systems

Play 20–30 runs. For each, ask:

  1. Did I care what Lucy was going to do?
  2. Did I understand why she did it?
  3. Did I want to try a different preparation?
  4. Did the run produce at least one “WHAT THE FUCK, LUCY” moment?
  5. Did I want a longer hallway for a new possibility, rather than a bigger number?

Mostly yes: there is a game. If 1 or 3 is no, do not add progression — fix the behaviour loop first.

Do not turn this design into a large systems implementation yet. The prototype needs exactly enough simulation to answer one question: can preparing Lucy actually produce interestingly different Lucy? If that works, the rest is cheap. If it doesn't, we learned it before spending thirty hours building a ferret-based spreadsheet.
