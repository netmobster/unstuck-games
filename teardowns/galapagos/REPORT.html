Tombstone for Ferret Bowling: Lucy Edition

GalápagosMendel's Escape · Anark · 1997

A 3D puzzle game where you never touch the hero. Mendel, a synthetic creature with a learning controller called NERM, walks where he likes. You change the world around him and hope he learns the right lesson.

A real learning creature, trapped inside a camera it couldn't escape.

01 · Summary firstThe verdict

Galápagos had the right idea and the wrong game around it. Everyone who played it agreed Mendel felt alive, and that watching him stop walking into a hazard he had died to was magic. Then the camera took the hazard out of view, he took forever to get anywhere, the learning was invisible, and a creature killed too often turned neurotic. The idea outlived the product.

For Lucy, the lesson is almost clean. Ferret Bowling already fixes the things that killed Galápagos, and it quietly makes one big move of its own.

Lift it

What Galápagos proved

  • Indirect control of a creature that looks alive is instantly compelling.
  • You shape the world, the creature does the rest: a "3D Lemmings with one lemming".
  • Personality from history (bold vs. cautious) is something players name and share.
  • Saving and trading a "brain" was its most loved feature.
Already fixed by the brief

What killed it

  • The automatic camera hid what mattered. Lucy's camera is fixed behind her.
  • Slow, long, punishing sessions. Lucy's runs are seconds long.
  • Death spirals into a broken creature. Lucy always comes back.
  • Tech first, game second. The brief tests fun before systems.
The sideways move

The learner changed

  • In Galápagos the creature learned, and players couldn't see it.
  • In Ferret Bowling Lucy is already perfect. The player learns Lucy.
  • That turns the invisible thing into the loop itself. It only works if cause and effect are readable.

03 · MechanicsHow it played

The player can
  • Left-click world objects to toggle or reverse them: switches, sliding platforms, force fields, jump pads, lifts. verified
  • Right-click Mendel's side to prod him left or right. verified
  • Fence him onto save pads and save (save-anywhere arrived in patch 1.1/1.2). verified
  • Save, load and export his brain, separately from level progress. verified
  • Pause any time to plan. claimed
The player cannot
  • Steer Mendel, point him at a goal, or warn him of danger. verified
  • Make Mendel use levers or switches; that job is the player's. verified
  • Control the camera. It moves on its own. verified
  • See his learning. No meter or display was found in any source. not found

Mendel

A spider-like synthetic creature, deliberately designed to look like no real animal so players would bring no assumptions about how he behaves. A newborn Mendel can't walk; the game ships a pre-trained brain of roughly eight hours claimed. He turns away when he senses something ahead, has no jump of his own, overshoots edges because his servos have inertia, and learns to avoid what hurts him verified. Lessons survive death. Too many deaths close together, somewhere he can't escape, and he turns neurotic; somewhere safe and stimulating, he recovers verified. Designers had to widen catwalks because he picked up a fear of heights verified.

Moment to moment
  1. Camera follows him into an area; you read what's clickable and what's lethal.
  2. He wanders and turns back at obstacles.
  3. You toggle objects to open paths, fence off wrong turns, set up launches.
  4. Prod him if he drifts the wrong way.
  5. He dies, re-forms at the last save, and has learned something.
  6. Try again with a slightly different creature.
World to world
  1. Clear the Galápagos hub puzzles.
  2. Enter a dimension: Sine, Plon, Hellen, Lumen.
  3. Chain puzzles between save pads, each world more disorienting.
  4. Return to a harder hub section.
  5. Finale: fire the gate cutter, the station self-destructs, Mendel escapes.

04 · Under the hoodInside NERM

Non-stationary Entropic Reduction Mapping (not "Machine"). Scott Collins built it as a general adaptive controller; the founders pitched it for industrial and telecom uses and said it was neither a neural network nor a genetic algorithm. There is no patent for it, no paper, and no public code. Everything below is the developers' own account verified.

Senses6 IR rays + touchCoarse left/right brightness. Hazards emit IR. Body height reshapes the beams.
ControllerStarts as noiseAn untrained mapping from senses to motors that looks random: a newborn flails.
SignalOne error numberMotor strain, IR exposure and collisions. Walls, falls and heat push it up.
LearningNever stopsReshapes itself to push error down, only in situations it has actually lived through.
Body3 servos, no animationEvery pose is computed from motor angles each frame; inertia makes him overshoot.
Design lesson for a deterministic browser game

What made Mendel feel alive wasn't the algorithm.

It was coarse perception, visible clumsiness turning into competence, and personality that built up from history. All three can be faked with seeded rules: a few sensed "buckets", weighted choices that sharpen after pain, seeded weighted picks instead of always choosing the best action (same situation, different choice, identical replays), and a small shareable state file. For Lucy the ask is even smaller: she doesn't need to learn, she needs to look like she has opinions.

What people loved
  • Mendel feels genuinely alive: he whimpers near danger and shrieks when threatened.
  • Watching him refuse a hazard that killed him is the hook everyone remembers.
  • Nothing else plays like it. "An inverted puzzle": you design, he plays.
  • Handsome, strange worlds; puzzles that stay fresh.
What drove them out
  • The camera, above all: it cut away from the thing you needed to click.
  • Slow, lumbering pace; long gaps between save points.
  • Learning that was inconsistent, invisible, or went wrong (walking in circles).
  • Hard to tell what was clickable; lots of wrist-heavy clicking.
  • A tech demo with too little reason to keep going.

Next Generation's verdict is the best summary of the relationship: at its best, after days of play, Mendel becomes a partner in solving puzzles; at its worst he's a crab dodging your clumsy clicks. It suits players with "time, patience, and imagination". A forum poster recalling it decades later remembered a Mendel that, after bad guidance, simply stopped moving, as if he'd learned not to trust them.

06 · TombstoneWhy it died

  1. Two years latePromised for early 1996, shipped late 1997. Creatures got the headline first.
  2. Scope cutTwelve worlds became five.
  3. The cameraNamed as the main flaw by every major review.
  4. Invisible learningThe core promise was hard to see, and sometimes went backwards.
  5. Slow and punishingLumbering pace, fixed save spots until a patch, a creature that could break.
  6. Tech in search of a gameNERM came first; the game was a low-risk showcase for it.
  7. The studio moved onAnark pivoted to tools in 1998; the NERM RPG never appeared.

07 · LineageThe other DNA

Learn to Fly · Light Bringer · 2009

Prep, launch, spend

A penguin, a ramp, six upgrades (ramp, acceleration, air resistance, glider, rockets, fuel), a 6,000 ft goal counted in days. Praised because each new piece of kit flips your strategy; criticized for a hard ceiling and early grind. The sequel's fixed-budget Arcade mode is a built-in fix for the upgrade treadmill; the third game was panned as grindy, with the best strategy collapsing to "point up". verified

Shopping Cart Hero · MonkeyWantBanana · 2009

Launch, trick, land

A timed run-up, a jump, flips in the air, then scored on distance and height. Upgrades: wheels, rockets, bought tricks, and "groupies", score multipliers you lose if you crash, a push-your-luck bet. Later sequels added themed worlds and bosses. verified

09 · For the next decision roundOpen questions

  1. Prep combination. One item per run, two with interactions, or separate behaviour axes? The evidence leans toward side-grades that bias choices, whatever the count.
  2. The one input. Does Ferret Bowling stay the only launcher with zero mid-run input, or does it get a single thing that isn't steering?
  3. What rolls reward. Distance and knocked-over things, or spectacle: chain reactions, rare interactions, "unscorable, worth it"?
  4. Sharing. Mendel's brain files were the most loved feature. Is a shareable run (seed + prep + floor) part of the free game?
  5. How Lucy shows mood mid-run. Only five rear-view poses exist. Tail, speed, sway and shadow can carry it; does anything else need art?
  6. The prototype's one question. Twin runs identical except one prep: can we measure that they diverge in a way a player can explain?
