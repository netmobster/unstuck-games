You never place a path. You place things, and the path is what is left. The engine redrew it the instant you sold or built, and its maker spent three patches stopping you from using that.

IThe verdict

The organ was never the towers

Desktop Tower Defense is remembered as the desk-mat game with the hand-drawn creeps. Underneath the desk is a different machine: there is no road. Creeps take the shortest open route from one edge to the other, and every tower you place is a wall that happens to shoot. The route is the negative space of your defence.

And the machine was live. Sell a tower and every creep on the board recomputed its path that tick. Players found that out within two months and called it juggling: open two exits, sell and rebuild one tile, and watch the creeps turn forever. Preece treated it as a bug. Version 1.2 added a score penalty for selling, 1.2c made selling progressively slower, 1.5 ships a challenge mode named "No sell (no juggling)".

The world remembered the desk. The thing worth digging up is the live-rerouting maze editor underneath it, and the fact that its author kept trying to switch it off.

IIIHow it played

The parts that matter here

  • A desk-mat grid. Creeps enter left and exit right. From Normal difficulty up, a second stream enters at the top and exits at the bottom, so the two routes cross in the middle. Flying waves ignore the maze.
  • No fixed path. Creeps take the shortest route and recompute whenever the layout changes. A placement that would seal every route is refused with the word "Blocking".
  • 50 waves, 20 lives, 80 gold. Gold per kill, no interest. A bonus for sending the next wave early.
  • Selling. Version 1.0 refunded in full (unverified); 1.2 added a score penalty; 1.2c slowed selling progressively; 1.5 refunds 75%. All of it aimed at juggling.
  • Seven towers. Pellet, Squirt, Dart, Swarm, Frost, Bash, Boost. Boost does not shoot; it multiplies its neighbours. The community converged on a compact central serpentine with towers set diagonally so creeps pass every face.

These details come from the research pass, not a full teardown. They get checked before anything here is quoted publicly.

IVWhy it died

Orphaned, not obsolete

Flash killed the client. The rights sat inside a company that had pivoted from a free Flash game to Facebook war games, and was then sold to a roll-up, so nobody with the rights had a reason to port it. The creators' own path, from DTD to Backyard Monsters to Battle Pirates, is the free-to-play pivot in person. The HTML5 versions that exist are fan emulation, not a new version.

VThe four beats

CREMATION
What burns

The desk, Flash, the fixed rectangle, towers as guns with ranges and tiers, gold-per-kill as the only economy, the leaderboard, juggling as an exploit.

TRANSPLANT
What survives

Place-and-reroute. The can't-seal rule. Live recomputation. Sell as a verb. Two streams that cross.

UNSTUCK
Where it lands

An endless hex plain. Every obstacle is a piece of the world with a denizen. Destroy is the attack, not the refund. The frontier keeps coming.

Cremation · what we refuse to reproduce

  • The desk and the Flash-era joke. The setting is the corpse's face. It stays dead.
  • Square cells and a fixed rectangle. We are hex, and the plain has no edge.
  • Towers as guns. Range circles, targeting modes, damage-per-second as the whole identity of a piece. A denizen has a behaviour, not a weapon.
  • Gold-per-kill as the only economy, and upgrade tiers one to five of the same gun. Tiles come from a draft.
  • Juggling as an exploit. We decide what mid-wave change means instead of inheriting the loophole and then the patches.
  • The leaderboard as the ending.

VIWhat nobody used properly

Live rerouting as a legitimate verb

DTD's engine recomputed every creep's path the instant you sold or placed a tower, and a crossing two-stream board made the reroute meaningful. Preece spent three patches penalising what that made possible. Fieldrunners, GemCraft, Infinitode and Mindustry all kept the maze static after placement and treated selling as a refund button. Nobody built the game where selling is the attack and the maze is meant to be rewritten under the creeps.

What Infinite Frontier takes

That idea goes at the centre, and it is where Jay's own pivot already pointed: destroy is one of four verbs, not a cleanup, and destruction feeds the next draft. Everything DTD never had is what keeps us at a distance from it: the plain has no edge, a destroyed thing leaves a mark, and the obstacle is a denizen rather than a gun.

One of four tests. The game earns the tombstone only if the other three turn out to matter: if swapping which denizen sits where barely changes a run, we have rebuilt DTD with biomes.

Our own DNA, from the library rather than the graveyard: Orbis (deterministic sim, invariants, sweeps, the causality probe) · The Last Warren (one set of modules for the page and the sweep runner; policies and a lookahead expert) · Elsewhere (unknown is not zero) · Bad Monkeys (generation and dice: pre-generate, lint, taste-sample).

VIIOpen questions

For the first prototype to answer, not to design in advance.

  1. Is juggling the game or the exploit? Three rules side by side: edit freely during waves; commit each enemy to its route at spawn; no editing during waves. Measure which one is a dominant strategy and which one is the best moment.
  2. What does "cannot seal" mean on a plain with no edge? from the tombstone DTD refused the sealing placement. Dungeon Keeper killed a gate by surrounding it. Invariant 1 or the DK marker; they cannot both hold.
  3. Do multiplying spawn points reproduce the crossing streams? from the tombstone DTD's Normal mode was interesting because two routes shared a middle. Our growth rule adds a spawn every two waves; the sweep should report how often routes cross.
  4. Is the refund too kind? DTD's 75% was still too much for its author. Ours is salvage that buys a reroll. The Settlers II marker says a destroyed thing should leave a scar.
  5. Does the denizen matter? Permute which denizen sits on which hex over the best layouts, hold everything else fixed, and see if survival moves. This is the test the whole pivot rests on.
  6. Flyers ignored the maze. from the tombstone What is our exceptional enemy, and does it ignore occupancy, effects, or neither?
