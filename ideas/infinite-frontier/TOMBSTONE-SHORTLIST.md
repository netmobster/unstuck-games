# INFINITE FRONTIER — tombstone shortlist

> Filed 2026-10-11, CC's research pass, run on Jay's "let's run it first, see what comes
> out." A shortlist for Jay to pick from, in the manner of The Last Warren's. Facts from
> web search only (Wikipedia and Steam were unreachable from this box), so anything quoted
> publicly gets checked in the teardown first. Unverified claims are marked.

## The organ, before the corpse

The brief says "the board is the build". Pulled apart, that is three different living
things, and each one has its own grave:

| | The organ | Where it was alive |
|---|---|---|
| **A** | **You draw the enemy's road.** The route is not given; it is the negative space of what you put down. | Desktop Tower Defense, Fieldrunners, Rampart |
| **B** | **Opening is attacking.** Growing the map is the thing that lets the enemy in. Expansion and wave are one verb. | Dungeon of the Endless |
| **C** | **What you don't pick becomes the terrain.** The draft is a maze-builder: the rejected offers are laid on the board as walls. | Gem TD and its descendants |

The brief has A as a tile grammar, B as a separate milestone cadence bolted beside a wave
timer, and C not at all. Picking the tombstone is picking which of these is the heart.
The other two can be markers.

The brief's accidental twins are **alive**, so they cannot be tombstones and we must be
visibly unlike them: **Rogue Tower** (2022, Die of Death Games, ~82% positive on Steam; the
path extends every wave and you influence where; cards after waves; unlocks between runs)
and **Isle of Arrows** (2022, Gridpop, ~80% positive; random tiles grow an island and decide
the enemy's route; last Steam update July 2023, so dormant but on sale). Also alive and
near: Loop Hero, Hexguardian, Monster Tiles TD.

## The shortlist

### 1. Dungeon of the Endless (2014) — organ B

| | |
|---|---|
| **Developer** | Amplitude Studios (Paris), published by Sega from 2016 |
| **Status** | Original still sold, ports to Switch/PS4/Xbox 2020. Amplitude's own successor, **Endless Dungeon** (Oct 2023), landed at ~65% positive on ~3,800 Steam reviews and an estimated ~114k units (third-party estimate). Sega sold Amplitude back to its management in **November 2024** as the end of its European restructuring. The successor is the tombstone's own sequel missing the mark, which is the Majesty pattern. |
| **Mourned** | A passionate fanbase that Amplitude itself says surprised them when the successor was announced; the Steam threads on Endless Dungeon are fans of the original saying it isn't the thing. |

**How it played, the parts that matter here.** A floor is a dark map of rooms behind
doors. You hold a crystal in the start room. **Dust** is scarce: you spend it to power
rooms, and a powered room must chain back to the crystal. Monsters **never spawn in a
powered room**; every time you **open a door**, every unpowered room may spawn, and they
come for your crystal and your heroes. You can almost never afford to power everything, so
the map you grow is always bigger than the map you can hold. To leave a floor, one hero
**picks up the crystal and carries it to the exit**, slowed, unable to fight, while every
unpowered room pours out infinitely. Leaving is the hardest thing in the game.

**What was alive.** *The act of expanding is the act of being attacked.* There is no wave
timer. The wave is the door you chose to open, and the consequence lands on the ground you
chose not to afford.

**Cremation.** Heroes with skill trees, the dungeon crawl, the sci-fi prison, items, the
crystal escort as a literal escort mission, floors as acts, co-op.

**Transplant.** Chunk placement **is** the wave: you attach a 9×9 chunk, and that is what
spawns the enemies. There is no "next wave" button in the game. Dust becomes the thing
that decides which chunks are *held* (powered, defended) and which are *conceded* (they
spawn). The run's end is a carry: to bank a run you move the core across the board you
built, and everything you conceded comes alive.

**Unstuck.** Put that organ into a top-down phone TD with no heroes, no crawl, no
inventory: a grid, a core, chunks, and one verb that is both "grow" and "fight". It
collapses the brief's two cadences into one and kills the slot-arithmetic problem, because
the question is never "where do I put this tower" but "which ground can I afford to hold".

**What nobody used properly.** Dungeon of the Endless buried "opening is attacking" under
a hero RPG and then its own successor turned it into a twin-stick shooter. Rogue Tower and
Isle of Arrows have the expanding board but keep a wave timer, so expansion is free. Nobody
has made growth itself the cost.

### 2. Desktop Tower Defense (2007) — organ A

| | |
|---|---|
| **Creator** | Paul Preece, Flash, launched March 2007; 15.7 million plays by July 2007 |
| **Status** | Flash died December 2020 and took it down. Preece and David Scott's Casual Collective (Dec 2007) ran a multiplayer "DTD Pro"; the company **rebranded as Kixeye** and pivoted to Facebook war games (Battle Pirates, War Commander), which is the exact F2P the studio homepage satirises. Per a community post (unverified), Kixeye kept DTD Pro up until near Flash's end, then handed the code to Scott. Today: a DS port (2009, panned as a paid copy of a free game) and an unofficial HTML5 version on CrazyGames. |
| **Mourned** | TIME's All-Time 100 Video Games (2012) credits it with popularising the whole genre. kottke.org, 2026: "one of the very best games from the Flash era." |

**How it played.** An open desktop. Creeps enter one side and leave the other by the
shortest route they can find. Towers are solid, so **the towers are the maze**; you cannot
seal the exit, so the skill is the longest, meanest corridor you can afford. Kills pay gold,
gold buys towers, towers lengthen the road.

**What was alive.** *The road is the negative space of your defence.* You never place a
path; you place walls that happen to shoot.

**Cremation.** The desktop joke, Flash, the fixed rectangle, gold-per-kill, upgrade tiers,
the leaderboard, and the juggling exploits.

**Transplant.** The chunk grammar goes: the route is not drafted, it emerges. Towers are
obstacles first and guns second. "Chunks" become more ground, not more path.

**Unstuck.** It is honestly a bigger pivot away from the brief than the others, and
Infinitode 2, Mindustry and the mazing-TD mobile genre are alive and crowded. Its best use
here may be as the marker that keeps us honest: *does the player ever get to draw, or only
to choose from three drawings?*

**What nobody used properly.** The creators themselves: they took the most generous game
of its era and became Kixeye. There is a studio-shaped joke in resurrecting it.

### 3. Gem TD (c. 2005, Warcraft III custom map) — organ C

| | |
|---|---|
| **Creator** | Credited to "Bryan K" on one remake page (unverified); standalone web version at gemtowerdefense.com by Peter Holko; descendants Alchemist TD (Java applet → HTML5), GEMAZE TD, Gem TD remakes on itch |
| **Status** | A WC3 custom map. Warcraft III Reforged (2020) wrecked the custom-map scene and its reputation; the map survives only as fan remakes and a web port. Still named alongside Legion TD, Element TD and Wintermaul as the best TD maps the engine produced. |

**How it played.** Each round you place **five gems**; each is a **random type and grade**
you only learn after placing. You **keep one** as a tower. **The other four turn to rock**,
and the rocks are your maze. Same-type gems combine into better ones. The route is whatever
the rocks leave open.

**What was alive.** *The draft and the maze are the same act.* Every offer you reject is
still on the board, as a wall. There are no dead cards, because a dead card is terrain.

**Cremation.** WC3, the gem recipe book, 50 fixed waves, the lobby.

**Transplant.** The brief's "three cards, take one" becomes "three cards, take one, the two
you leave are laid where you looked at them": as blocks, slow ground, a bend in the path. It
directly answers the brief's slot-arithmetic problem (REVIEW §A): a tower offer with nowhere
to go still changes the board.

**Unstuck.** As a tombstone it is a smaller corpse than the other two. As a **marker** it is
possibly the single best idea on this list.

### 4. Rampart (1990, arcade) — organ A, with a clock

| | |
|---|---|
| **Developer** | Atari Games, John Salwitz and Dave Ralston; limited release Oct 1990, wide Feb 1991 |
| **Status** | Thirteen ports (SNES, Genesis, Lynx, NES, GB...), a PS3 network version, Midway Arcade Treasures (2004). Its rights went with Atari Games to Midway and then to **Warner Bros.** in Midway's bankruptcy, which is why it is **absent from Atari 50**. Nobody owns the job of keeping it alive. |
| **Mourned** | "People describe it as either an early version of Tower Defense, or Missile Command meets Tetris... there's never been a game like it since." |

**How it played.** Build phase: Tetris-shaped wall pieces, a timer, enclose your castle and
as much ground as you can; what you enclose is what you own and where cannons may stand.
Battle: shoot the ships, they shoot holes in your wall. Repair phase: a hole you can't close
before the clock is a castle you lose.

**What was alive.** *Damage is a hole in your build, and you rebuild under the clock.* The
board is the build, literally, and the enemy edits it.

**Transplant.** Chunks or towers can be destroyed, leaving holes; the draft between waves is
partly a repair. Enemies attack the route, not just the core.

**Unstuck.** A strong marker. As a tombstone the organ is the clock and the enclosure, and
the brief's game is not about enclosure.

### 5. Fieldrunners (2008, iPhone) — organ A on a phone

| | |
|---|---|
| **Developer** | Subatomic Studios; their first game, 5 Oct 2008; IGF Mobile 2009 Best Game; TIME top 10 of 2008 |
| **Status** | Alive but barely: the original went four years without an update until a 64-bit patch for iOS 11; Fieldrunners 2 (2012) shed maps in its own 64-bit update per user reviews. Not delisted (I was wrong about that in the first review). |

**What was alive.** Open-field mazing with a thumb, on a 3.5-inch screen, in 2008. The proof
that organ A works on the device Jay is testing on.

**Use.** Marker, not tombstone: *the first mazing TD that fit in a hand.*

## CC's read

The organ the brief most wants and least has is **B**. Dungeon of the Endless is the
corpse that already had it, is mourned by name, and its own successor is the "missed the
mark" evidence the method asks for. Taking it collapses the brief's wave-timer-plus-milestone
structure into one verb and dissolves the slot-arithmetic problem rather than patching it.
Gem TD's rejected-cards-become-terrain is the marker that fixes the draft. Rampart and
Fieldrunners flag the clock and the thumb. Rogue Tower and Isle of Arrows stay on the record
as the living twins we must not be.

A possible graveyard record, if Jay agrees:

```
THE GRAVEYARD
Tombstone:  Dungeon of the Endless (2014)
Markers:    Gem TD — what you don't pick becomes the terrain
            Desktop Tower Defense — the road is the negative space of your defence
            Rampart — damage is a hole in your build; rebuild under the clock
            Fieldrunners — mazing that fit in a hand, 2008
DNA only:   Rogue Tower / Isle of Arrows — the living twins; stay unlike them
            Carcassonne — tile grammar, committed placement (alive)
            Slay the Spire — the three-card beat (alive)
```

But Jay picks. If the pick is **A**, the brief's tile grammar goes and the game becomes a
draw-your-own-maze TD with chunks as ground, up against a living genre. If **C**, the game
is a draft game first and a TD second, and the chunk cadence probably goes.
